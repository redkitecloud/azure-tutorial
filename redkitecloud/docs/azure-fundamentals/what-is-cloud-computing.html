The Definition of Cloud Computing

The most widely accepted definition comes from NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology):

📖
NIST Definition Cloud computing is a model for enabling ubiquitous, convenient, on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources — networks, servers, storage, applications, and services — that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort or service provider interaction.

That's a mouthful. In plain English: Cloud computing means renting computers, storage, and software over the internet — and paying only for what you use.

Instead of buying your own servers, building a data centre, and hiring staff to maintain it — you log into Azure, click a few buttons, and your infrastructure is ready in minutes. Scale it up when you need more. Scale it down when you don't. Pay only for what you consumed.

A Real-World Analogy

Think about electricity. You don't build your own power plant to get electricity at home. You connect to the grid, use as much as you need, and pay for what you consumed at the end of the month.

Cloud computing works exactly the same way — but for computing power, storage, and software.

Electricity (Power Grid)Cloud Computing
Power plant generates electricityData centre hosts servers
You connect via power linesYou connect via the internet
Use as much power as you needUse as much compute as you need
Pay a monthly electricity billPay a monthly cloud bill
No need to own a generatorNo need to own physical servers
💡
Real Examples You Already Use Gmail stores your emails on Google's cloud servers — not on your laptop. Netflix streams video from cloud servers. Swiggy, Zomato, and every major app you use runs on cloud infrastructure. You've been using the cloud for years without realising it.

The 5 Essential Characteristics

NIST defines 5 essential characteristics that every cloud service must have. These are heavily tested on the AZ-900 exam — memorise them.

1. On-Demand Self-Service

You can provision resources yourself — without calling anyone, raising a ticket, or waiting for approval. Go to the Azure Portal, click a few buttons, and your virtual machine is running in under 2 minutes.

💡
Example A developer needs a test server at 10 PM on Friday. With cloud, they spin up a VM themselves in 2 minutes. Without cloud, they'd raise a ticket and wait until Monday.

2. Broad Network Access

Cloud resources are accessible over the network from any device — laptop, mobile, tablet — from anywhere in the world. You don't need to be in a specific office or connected to a specific network.

3. Resource Pooling

The cloud provider pools computing resources and serves multiple customers from the same physical infrastructure. You share the underlying hardware with thousands of other customers — but each customer's data and workloads are completely isolated from one another.

ℹ️
Multi-tenancy This is called multi-tenancy. Think of it like an apartment building — many families share the same building, but each flat is private and secure. Azure's hypervisor technology ensures complete isolation between tenants on shared hardware.

4. Rapid Elasticity

Resources can be scaled up or down rapidly — sometimes automatically — to match demand. If your app suddenly gets 10x the normal traffic, Azure can add more servers within seconds. When traffic drops, it scales back down and you stop paying for the extra capacity.

💡
Example During a major online sale, an e-commerce platform can go from 10 servers to 200 servers automatically in minutes — then scale back down when the sale ends. Without cloud, they'd have to buy 200 servers and leave most of them idle for the rest of the year.

5. Measured Service

Cloud systems automatically monitor, measure, and report resource usage. You get full transparency into what you've used — and you pay only for that. Like a utility meter on your electricity or water — measured to the second.

What Did We Do Before the Cloud?

To understand why cloud is such a big deal, you need to understand what life was like before it. Every company had to build and manage their own IT infrastructure — called on-premises (on-prem).

  • Purchase physical servers weeks or months in advance
  • Build or rent a data centre with power, cooling, and physical security
  • Install and configure operating systems and software
  • Hire a team to monitor and maintain the hardware 24/7/365
  • Provision for peak demand — even if you only hit that peak once a year
  • Replace ageing hardware every 3–5 years
⚠️
The Core Problem If you under-provisioned, your website crashed under load. If you over-provisioned, you wasted millions on idle hardware. Estimating correctly was nearly impossible. Cloud computing eliminated this problem entirely.

CapEx vs OpEx

This is one of the most important concepts for the AZ-900 exam. Understand it deeply.

Capital Expenditure (CapEx)

CapEx is a large upfront investment in physical infrastructure. Buying servers, building a data centre, purchasing networking equipment — all CapEx. The value depreciates over time. You own the asset.

Operational Expenditure (OpEx)

OpEx is ongoing spending on services billed as you go. Cloud computing is OpEx. You pay a monthly bill for what you consumed — like your phone bill. No upfront investment, no owned assets, full flexibility to stop anytime.

CapEx (On-Premises)OpEx (Cloud)
Payment modelLarge upfront investmentPay-as-you-go monthly billing
OwnershipYou own the hardwareProvider owns the hardware
FlexibilityFixed capacity — hard to changeScale instantly, up or down
MaintenanceYour team's responsibilityProvider handles it
Time to deployWeeks to monthsMinutes
RiskHigh — easy to over/under provisionLow — pay for actual usage
💡
AZ-900 Exam Tip The exam loves CapEx vs OpEx. Key phrase to remember: "The ability to trade upfront capital expense for variable operational expense" is one of the defining benefits of cloud computing. Cloud = OpEx.

Key Benefits of Cloud Computing

High Availability

Azure operates globally distributed data centres with redundant power, networking, and hardware. Most services come with SLAs of 99.9% to 99.99% uptime — meaning your application stays online even when hardware fails underneath it.

Scalability

Two types of scaling:

  • Vertical scaling (Scale Up/Down) — Add more CPU or RAM to an existing server. Like upgrading your laptop from 8GB to 32GB RAM.
  • Horizontal scaling (Scale Out/In) — Add more servers to share the load. Like opening more checkout lanes at a supermarket when the queues get long.

Elasticity

Configure rules to automatically add or remove resources based on real-time demand — without any manual action. "Add a VM when CPU exceeds 80%. Remove it when CPU drops below 20%."

Agility

Deploy new infrastructure in minutes. A startup can go from idea to production environment in an afternoon — no procurement, no capital approval, no data centre setup required.

Geo-Distribution

Deploy your application to data centres across the globe with a few clicks. Azure has data centres in Central India, South India, and West India — plus 60+ regions worldwide. Users are served from the nearest location, dramatically reducing latency.

Disaster Recovery

Replicate your data and applications to a secondary region automatically. If one region fails, traffic fails over to another region — often without any downtime. Building this on-premises used to cost millions and take months. In Azure, it's a configuration option.

When you move to the cloud, security and management responsibilities are split between you and the cloud provider. This split depends on which type of cloud service you're using.
